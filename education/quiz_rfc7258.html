Q1: Treating pervasive monitoring as an attack primarily means

Multiple Choice
**Explanation:** **Why it matters:** RFC 7258 changes the technical baseline for protocol design. Its position is neither a legal statement nor a passive-eavesdropper-only model. **Terms:** **Pervasive monitoring (PM)** is indiscriminate, very large-scale collection of protocol artifacts. It can use active or passive wiretaps, traffic analysis, and subversion of cryptographic keys. **Correct (B):** The IETF treats PM as a technical attack on privacy and seeks protocol designs that make it significantly more expensive or infeasible where possible. **Options:** - A (incorrect): RFC 7258 distinguishes pervasive monitoring from beneficial monitoring such as network management. The technical assessment does not require eliminating every inspection activity. - B (correct): It captures the defining scale, the collection of content or metadata, the technical attack classification, and the qualified mitigation goal. - C (incorrect): The RFC's model includes active and passive wiretaps, traffic analysis, and even subversion of cryptographic keys; it is not limited to payload eavesdropping. **Related:** “Mitigate” does not mean completely prevent. A design can be worthwhile if it raises cost, makes covert collection overt, or improves detectability.

Q2: Design choices that generally reduce exposure to pervasive monitoring include (select all)

Multi-Select
**Explanation:** **Why it matters:** Content encryption is important, but RFC 7258 also identifies protocol metadata, traffic analysis, and data minimization as separate concerns. **Terms:** **Protocol metadata** includes information such as headers and identifiers. **Traffic analysis** infers information from correlation, timing, or packet sizes. **Data minimization** avoids exposing information that the protocol does not need. **Correct (A,B,C):** These choices reduce the artifacts available for indiscriminate collection and make correlation more difficult. **Options:** - A (correct): Protecting content in transit reduces direct disclosure. - B (correct): Encrypted payloads can still leak useful structure through metadata and observable traffic patterns. - C (correct): Unnecessary data and stable identifiers increase linkability without helping the core protocol function. - D (incorrect): A stable cleartext identifier makes sessions and users easier to correlate. The observer's motivation does not change that technical exposure. **Related:** RFC 7258 does not prescribe one fixed mechanism for every protocol. Designers must explain whether PM is relevant and justify the architectural decisions they make.

Q3: Which statement best describes what encryption contributes against pervasive monitoring

Multiple Choice
**Explanation:** **Why it matters:** Encryption is essential in many PM mitigations, but treating it as a complete solution hides the information that remains observable. **Real-world usage:** Protocol and privacy reviews use this separation to check content protection, metadata exposure, endpoint security, and key management independently. **Terms:** **Traffic analysis** draws inferences from timing, volume, and packet sizes. **Endpoint compromise** exposes information before encryption or after decryption. **Correct (C):** Encryption can hide content from observers, but it does not automatically hide metadata, eliminate correlation, authenticate peers, or protect compromised endpoints. **Options:** - A (incorrect): Authentication and endpoint integrity require separate properties and controls. - B (incorrect): Observable endpoints, timing, sizes, and identifiers can remain even when the payload is encrypted. - C (correct): It states both the benefit and the residual exposure. **Related:** A useful PM analysis explicitly lists what becomes confidential and what an observer can still learn.

Q4: Your protocol sends user identifiers in cleartext to allow caching. Under the RFC 7258 mindset, what is a reasonable next step

Multiple Choice
**Explanation:** **Why it matters:** A cleartext identifier can be convenient for caching or analytics while also making large-scale correlation cheap. **Terms:** **Linkability** is the ability to connect separate protocol events to the same user, device, or relationship. **Correct (A):** The design should revisit whether the identifier is necessary and whether a less exposed or less stable representation can meet the functional requirement. **Options:** - A (correct): It lowers correlation value while preserving room to meet the caching requirement. - B (incorrect): Benign intent does not reduce the identifier's value to an observer; its stability still links events. - C (incorrect): Replacing a name with an equally stable pseudonym can preserve linkability, so it does not by itself prevent correlation. **Related:** RFC 7258 asks designers to make this trade-off early because architectural exposure is expensive to remove after deployment.

Q5: Which statement best captures the document's intent

Multiple Choice
**Explanation:** **Why it matters:** RFC 7258 establishes a design responsibility; it does not mandate one universal privacy technology or regulate monitoring equipment. **Terms:** An **architectural decision** includes the selection and placement of protocol mechanisms that can expose or protect content and metadata. **Correct (B):** IETF specifications should consider whether PM is relevant and, when it is, be able to justify related design decisions. **Options:** - A (incorrect): The RFC explicitly says a separate section is not required, and it does not mandate one mechanism for every protocol. - B (correct): It states the relevance test and the responsibility to explain how related design decisions were made. - C (incorrect): Metadata, traffic analysis, key subversion, and architectural exposure remain technical design concerns even when payload content is encrypted. **Related:** The RFC says a separate “pervasive monitoring considerations” section is not required; what is required is a good answer about relevance and design treatment.

Q6: What kind of data can remain exposed and still enable large scale correlation even when payload is encrypted (one word)

Short Text
**Explanation:** **Why it matters:** Payload confidentiality does not remove all information that can support surveillance and correlation. **Terms:** **Metadata** includes protocol headers, endpoints, identifiers, sizes, timing, and routing information that describe a communication rather than its application content. **Correct:** **metadata**. **Why this matters:** At large scale, metadata can reveal relationships, behavior, and activity patterns even when the content is not readable. **Related:** PM review should examine content, metadata, traffic-analysis signals, and key-management assumptions separately.

Q7: Which logging decision best follows the design reasoning of RFC 7258

Multiple Choice

An agent platform proposes retaining task IDs, tenant IDs, key fingerprints, and session proofs for 30 days in a centrally searchable log to support incident investigation.

**Explanation:** RFC 7258 classifies **pervasive monitoring**—indiscriminate, very large-scale collection of protocol artifacts—as an attack. A log with a legitimate operational purpose can still become a data source for correlating actors, tenants, and tasks when identifiers are aggregated and retained. - A (incorrect): A security purpose does not remove exposure to compromise, insider misuse, secondary use, or future correlation. “Might be useful later” is not a data-minimization rule. - B (incorrect): RFC 7258 does not prohibit all beneficial monitoring, such as network management or attack detection. Making a system unmanageable is not its intended outcome. - C (correct): Field-level necessity, reduced linkability, bounded access, limited retention, and redaction address both operational value and the risk that monitoring facilities can be abused for PM. The draft applies this boundary to full grants, session proofs, key fingerprints, tenant IDs, and task IDs: avoid recording them by default, and apply retention limits, access controls, and redaction when a security audit requires logging. Logging is not automatically the attack; it is an **observation and correlation surface** that belongs in the threat model.
